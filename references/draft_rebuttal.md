# 6CNs Reviewer Rebuttal (Rating 3)

We thank the reviewer for their praise of our results, and address their concerns below:


> Human annotation is included but is limited to a small number of samples

The relatively small number of annotations in our setup is a deliberate choice, reflecting our alignment with established practices in psychometrics. In psychometric research, annotations are typically produced by domain experts (in our case, two trained psychologist and an experienced police officer) using carefully designed frameworks to ensure that each label faithfully captures the underlying construct of interest. This emphasis on conceptual precision and measurement validity naturally constrains volume but results in higher-quality annotations. In contrast, common machine learning pipelines often rely on large-scale, crowd-sourced labeling by non-experts to maximize coverage and statistical power.

> inter-rater agreement reported in Cohen’s kappa is only about 0.63 with some dimensions as low as 0.33

Cohen’s κ deflates under near-ceiling distributions [1] — a known issue on 5-point scales where raters cluster near the top. Average criterion scores are 4.72/5 across all rubrics, producing exactly these conditions. Human vs GPT Mean Absolute Deviation (Tables 5, 8) is more interpretable: persona MAD 0.56/5 (range 0.14–1.45, Table 5) and SJT MAD 0.32/5 (range 0.00–1.17, Table 8) — both well below one scale point out of 5. For the HEXACO trait-alignment rubrics specifically — the dimensions most central to our validity claims — Human vs GPT MAD averages 0.10/5 with four of six traits at exactly 0.00, indicating strong practical agreement on what matters most.

[1] Feinstein, A. R., & Cicchetti, D. V. (1990). High agreement but low kappa: I. The problems of two paradoxes. *Journal of Clinical Epidemiology*, 43(6), 543–549.


> The primary focus on Gemma-3-4b-Instruct for main results limits generalization claims across model families.

In addition to Gemma-3-4b-Instruct, we have replicated the results on Qwen/Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct as well yielding similar trait score distributions. We accept that we can highlight the results of the Qwen model in a better way in the main body, and we will correct that in a camera ready or future revisions.

We acknowledge that releasing a dataset of 12,000 synthetic police officer personas and over 4 million role-played responses introduces both opportunities for responsible research and risks of misuse. On the positive side, it enables systematic study of behavioral consistency, bias propagation, and trait-conditioned reasoning in LLMs under controlled settings without relying on real human data. At the same time, we will emphasize that all personas are synthetic abstractions and do not correspond to real individuals and the dataset is not intended for deployment in decision-making pipelines, and any observed correlations between traits and behaviors should not be interpreted as evidence of real-world psychological validity.

To mitigate misuse, we will incorporate access control tiering in the dataset release: a public subset will be openly available, while full dataset access will require a lightweight application and agreement to a research-use license prohibiting high-risk applications such as individual profiling, surveillance, or automated decision-making about real people. The release will also include a detailed dataset card specifying intended use cases, explicitly disallowed uses, and key limitations, including risks of representational bias. We will incorporate these changes in the camera-ready or future versions of the dataset release.

> Finally, the HEXACO framework assumes personality traits map cleanly to decisions, but the "trait bleed" problem the authors try to mitigate shows this mapping is actually quite noisy even after refinement.

In our SJT design, we adhere to established psychometric principles, ensuring that each response option primarily reflects the intended trait while minimizing trait bleed. To validate this mapping, the SJTs were reviewed by two experienced psychologists both before and after the trait-bleed mitigation process. Their evaluations indicate that our approach substantially reduces trait bleed and improves construct validity in the final SJT set.

# PpFE Reviewer Rebuttal (Rating 3.5)

We thank the reviewer for their praise of our results, and address their concerns below:

> The evidence is therefore strongest for stable and interpretable model behavior, not for human-grounded psychological validity.

We agree with the reviewer that our pipeline provides evidence for stable and interpretable model behavior rather than human-grounded psychological validity, and we emphasize that this is an intentional design choice. Our goal is not to approximate human psychological traits, as this risks anthropomorphizing language models, but to use “traits” as controlled behavioral abstractions for systematically probing model responses under different persona-conditioned settings. Within this framing, we focus on measuring the consistency and structure of trait-aligned behaviors across scenarios, without claiming correspondence to real-world psychological constructs. The use of synthetic personas, scenarios, and automated judgments enables controlled, reproducible analysis, and we have mentioned this positioning in the paper as well, to underscore that the framework is a diagnostic tool for studying model behavior rather than a proxy for human psychometrics.

> The current SJT setup is still mostly single-turn. This is acceptable for the present contribution, but longitudinal or interactive SJTs would be important for future work.

We agree with the reviewer that extending the SJT setup to longitudinal or interactive settings is an important direction and as noted in the limitations section, we view multi-turn and longitudinal SJTs as a natural next step, allowing investigation of how trait-aligned behaviors evolve over extended dialogues, including stability, drift, and context-dependent modulation


> The authors should continue to sharpen the distinction between “personality traits” as human psychological constructs and “trait-aligned behavioral tendencies” as observed in model outputs.

We agree that the distinction between human “personality traits” and model-expressed behaviors should be made more explicit. In our work, we use the notion of traits strictly as trait-aligned behavioral tendencies observed in model outputs, rather than as claims about human psychological constructs. We do not intend to ascribe human-like personality or psychological validity to the models, but instead use traits as controlled abstractions for probing behavior under different conditions. We will sharpen this distinction in the camera-ready or future version by clarifying our terminology.
